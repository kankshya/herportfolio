A thing I would like people to know about me is that I love it here when I am doing meaningful work and continue learning along the way. What I value most in graphic design and communication is expression, accessible knowledge, instinctive surprises, and endless curiosity. I completed my Bachelor’s in Multimedia and Mass Communication from Indraprastha College for Women, University of Delhi, in 2020, where I also studied sociology for four semesters as part of my degree. In 2025, I moved to London to pursue an MA in Design for Visual Communication at the London College of Communication. Alongside my work as a visual communication designer, I have continued to practise and perform classical dance under the tutelage of the renowned dancer and choreographer Madhavi Mudgal. I am quite fascinated by music and sculpture, neither of which I practise, but I think the contrast between the rigidity of one and the fluidity of the other continually inspires me.

[My dancers] are all pearls. Each in his own way, each in a different form. I find it beautiful, when at the end of a performance you feel a little bit closer to them, because they have showed something of themselves. That is something very real.

The Missing Masses of a Design School

The Missing Masses of a Design School asks whether graphic design can produce knowledge rather than merely communicate it. Moving through rhetoric as epistemology, praxis, material rhetoric, and Bruno Latour’s “missing masses,” the book examines the often-overlooked objects, tools, materials, and practices that participate in how designers think and learn. Developed through a year-long inquiry during my MA, the book brings together an essay with conversations with Martin Lorenz and Deborah Littlejohn. Across these chapters, it looks towards the overlooked objects, tools, materials, and practices of the design school to consider how designers come to know through making, and how design pedagogy might cultivate criticality as part of practice rather than leave it dependent on cultural capital.

Scraps on the Way Home

“What if the scraps from my project end up in a landfill at home?” was the looming question that led to this installation, made in response to UAL’s Earth Week open call for 2026. The workshop apron, as a metaphor for the embodiment of the creative process, suggests an unseen guilt: the suspicion that waste may be offloaded onto places closer to home once it enters commercial UK recycling channels, which remain heavily reliant on countries in the Global South—many of them where the climate crisis is already a lived reality.

The work uses gelli prints taken from impressions of the apron itself, turning its stains, folds, and wear into repeating visual traces. These printed marks become material evidence of making, hinting at the hidden afterlife of studio waste.

At the centre, a projection-mapped apron displays moving images of scraps collected from across the college, layering real leftover materials back onto the garment. This creates a loop between body, tool, and waste, asking where responsibility for artistic leftovers truly lies.

Distilled / Economic and Political Weekly

As a Multimedia Content Visualiser at Economic and Political Weekly (EPW), I have been dedicated to making academic knowledge and information more accessible to a wider audience. One of the key initiatives I have been involved in is EPW Engage, which aims to bridge the gap between complex academic ideas and everyday understanding.

A woman in wolf’s clothing

This feature examines the role of caste in shaping the wage gap narrative, focusing on the dynamics with caste group horizontal. While the nature of employment and wage is associated with education level, the same may not always help in getting a better-paying job. Therefore, the project visualises the wage gap across various types of occupations per caste group. This infographic is based on the article “Caste and Wage Inequalities in India” by Praveen Kumar and Indervir Singh published in EPW.

The Ocean has Begun Misprinting the Seashells

Ocean Acidification is often said to be climate change’s equally evil twin because it is driven by the same excess carbon dioxide, but its damage unfolds out of sight beneath the surface.This data story turns that hidden process into visible form. Using yearly pH data, a generative system slowly warps, wobbles, and drifts shell shapes away from a 1985 baseline. As seawater acidifies, creatures like urchins, mussels, and oysters struggle to build strong shells. Here, the ocean acts like a RISO machine, producing increasing misregistration as a visualization of how changing chemistry reshapes the shells.
